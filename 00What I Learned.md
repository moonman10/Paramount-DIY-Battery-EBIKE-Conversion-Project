I learned a lot because of this project. I started this project knowing very little about the layout of a battery pack, how it is put together, how it works with the controller to spin the rotor, and how it uses CC-CV charging to safely restore voltage. But over the course of this summer, I became very familiar with all of these things.

For the layout of the battery pack, I learned that the cells are organized into parallel groups by connecting the negative ends and positive ends together and then linking those parallel groups up in series. The more parallel groups that you have in series, the more the voltage of your battery will increase. The more cells that you add in parallel within your parallel groups, the more current your battery can discharge. Your battery pack also has a greater capacity which is measured in amp hours. Amp hours are basically just how many amps that your battery can output for one hour. I learned that these groupings are connected with nickel strips that are need to be made to fit the layout of your battery pack. The battery pack also includes a battery management system that connects one balance wire to each one of the parallel groups. The separate port BMS that I used also has three larger wire ports labeled P-, B- and C-. P- connects to the negative power wire of the battery, B- connects to the battery pack negative nickel strip tab, and C- connects to the negative charging wire. The positive end of the battery connects to the positive charging port terminal, a balance wire measuring that last cell group, and the positive power wire, which connects with the inline fuse going out the casing to the controller.

I learned how these packs are put together. You should always check the starting voltage of all the cells that you use. You also need to have the correct casing for your battery. This casing usually comes with a honeycomb layout that you can place the cells into. The next step is to use a spot welder to weld the nickel strips onto the cells in the correct orientation. This is how the parallel groupings are formed and connected in series with one another. The next step is to figure out where you are going to place your BMS. You need to make sure that you have enough space within the casing to fit your BMS and that all of the wires connected to the BMS can reach their destinations. Then you can start to solder on the BMS balance wires. And after that the rest of the wiring. Don’t forget to install the inline fuse by cutting out a section of the positive power wire and soldering on the fuse wire. Then make sure to secure the balance wire connector to the BMS. You also need to insulate the entire battery using insulator tape which including all connections, cells, and the BMS. Then you can secure the battery pack within the case. 

I learned about how this design works to deliver power to the motor. First of all, you have a consistent DC source coming from the battery through the power wires that connects to the controller. The controller can use this DC source to create a low frequency AC through the 3 phase wires running from the controller to the motor. So basically the controller can connect the positive power wire of the battery to any of the three phase wires, labeled A, B and C, and the negative power wire to any of the three phase wires as well. However, the positive and negative of the battery must never be connected to the same phase wire because this would create a shoot through short circuit and could destroy the controller. So for example if A was connected to the positive of a 46.8V battery and C was connected to the negative, then the voltage across A and C would be 46.8V. But then the ESC could flip that and connect C to the positive and A to the negative. So between A and C there is now -46.8V. The motor needs this swap because it relies on the magnetic field of the coils on the stator to be reserving in polarity in order to keep the magnets of the rotor rotating. Furthermore, while the controller is outputting current in a single phase (same polarity between two phase wires), it also has the ability to use PWM to increase or decrease the average voltage between those two phase wires. So the general workflow when you press the throttle is that you send a signal to the controller that opens certain MOSFET switches, allowing the phase wires to be connected to the batteries DC supply in a very specific way. This is what allows the rotor to spin due to the switching of the polarity of the magnetic fields coming from the coils in the stator that current runs through. So the batteries main job is to store and supply that constant power source. It does this by using its individual lithium ion cells that can convert chemical potential energy into electrical current that flows through the pack and the wires connected to it. So when your battery has that chemical potential energy built up, and the controller connects the battery pack to the motor using high frequency PWM DC and low frequency AC, the conventional current runs from positive end of the pack to the controller, to the phase wires, to the motor windings, back through a different phase wire, back through the controller, back to the pack, through the P- port of the BMS, and out of the B- port of the BMS to the negative end of the battery pack, completing the circuit. The BMS monitors the current on the battery side of the controller. This current is controlled by the controller using PWM conducted by its MOSFETs. The higher the PWM, the higher the current leaving the pack will be. If the current leaving the pack gets too high, the BMS will shut down the pack. If the voltage of one of the cell groups gets too low, the BMS will shut down the pack. The BMS has a temperature sensor and if the temperature gets too high, the BMS will shut down the pack. 

Lastly, the charging works by using CC-CV charging. CC-CV stands for constant current-constant voltage. The charger is connected to the positive side of the pack and the C- port of the BMS via the positive and negative charge wires. The C- port is connected within the BMS to the B- port which is connected to the pack negative completing the circuit. The first phase is the constant current phase. The idea is to hold the output current of the charger steady while the voltage of the battery pack slowly rises. Once the battery pack voltage meets the chargers voltage, which for my pack is 54.6 V, the charger switches to constant voltage mode. The idea of this mode is to hold the voltage of the pack steady while the current coming from the charger slowly decreases. This is necessary because if you keep trying to force the same current into the battery cells when the battery is already at a high voltage, the cells could easily become overcharged and this could damage them. But you still need a bit of current to ensure that the max voltage of each cell is fully reached. The voltage at the terminal of the cells is a bit higher than the voltage of the actual cell. This is because the voltage at the terminal is a sum of both the actual cell voltage and the product of the current running through the cell and the internal cell resistance (IR). So decreasing current means that you can slowly get the actual cell voltage to the max, while gradually eliminating that added voltage coming from the IR.
