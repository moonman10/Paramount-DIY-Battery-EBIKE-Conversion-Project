The first problem I encountered was that when I fit the battery pack inside the case, I didn’t have enough insulator tape around the thin BMS balance wire connections. This caused one of these wires to break where it connects to the white BMS connector. I had to order a whole new BMS in order to get a new set of balance wires that I could snap into the BMS. So I desoldered all of the old BMS balance wires and re-soldered the new ones. The next time I tried to fit the battery into the casing, I made sure to protect the weak BMS balance wire connections with insulator tape and take my time squeezing the battery into the case.

Next, I encountered a huge charging issue that could have been fatal to my project. I made a mistake when soldering the charging wires. The charging port I used was a 3-pin XLR port, and so there were three pins available for wiring the negative and positive charging wires. I did some research online on 3-pin XLR ports, and there was a lot of information. I thought that I had found the correct pinout diagram for it, but the diagram that I used had the negative and positive pins flipped. So when I tried to charge the battery, of course it didn’t work, and this also could have seriously damaged the battery. Luckily, all it did was break the two chargers I was using. To fix this, I had the idea of checking the correct polarity of the charger pins by plugging the charger into the wall and using a multimeter to measure the voltage across the pins. One charger still showed 54.6 volts when plugged into the wall. The polarity came out opposite to what I was expecting, and so that’s when I realized what the issue was. I re-soldered the charging wires, and when I tried charging it, things still didn’t work because the charger was broken. This is when I realized that the wiring mistake had broken the chargers. So I had to order a new charger, and then the charging worked flawlessly. This really taught me not to trust online diagrams blindly and to always validate the polarity and power path within the design myself.

The next problem I had was with the controller bike mounts. The cylindrical mounts that came with the controller for installation were not large enough to fit around the downtube and were even a tad loose around the seat tube. However, they definitely fit better around the seat tube. So I decided to mount the controller to the seat tube within the main bike triangle. This caused an issue with leaving enough space for the front derailleur. So I was not able to install front shifting on the bike. I didn’t view this as a huge issue, however, because with the chain on the largest front gear and a middle gear on the back, the pedals had a reasonable amount of resistance for pedaling. This is good for riding on flat ground. With the motor, all you really need for a comfortable ride is that single gear. You get more assistance from the pedal assist than you ever would from pedaling alone anyway. So I decided that there wasn’t much of a point in installing front shifting on the bike. I also would have had to spend more on a few key shifting components that I did not have.
